Welcome!

I am a PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Exeter.

Research area: I am an applied microeconomist working on topics in labour, gender and development economics.

Job Market Paper

The Withholding Edge: Strategic Sharing of Labour-Market Information

(Draft coming soon)

Abstract (AEA Registry)
Job seekers often learn about opportunities through peers, but knowing that a vacancy exists is different from knowing how to succeed in its selection process. This paper asks whether peers pass on both types of information when doing so may weaken their own chances of being hired. I study this question in a lab-in-the-field experiment with 518 university students in Islamabad, Pakistan, using a real paid job and pre-existing friendship networks. I cross-randomise access to process information, the observability of sharing, and the salience of competition. Participants choose whether to share the vacancy and, when informed, which of those same peers also receive the process information. Among the links through which informed participants share the vacancy, 27\% do not carry process information. A reminder of competition reduces the probability of sharing process information by 15 percentage points, or 35\% of its mean of 43.2\%, despite changing nothing about the job. Withholding is rarely targeted at particular recipients; only 7\% of informed sharers give process information to some, but not all, of the peers with whom they share the vacancy. Pre-existing friendship, rather than peers’ academic standing or perceived hiring prospects, predicts who receives vacancy information. These findings show that peer networks can spread awareness of jobs while limiting access to information that helps applicants compete. Giving information to some jobseekers therefore need not ensure that it reaches others through existing ties.

Selected work in progress

Women and Corporate Boards: Network Centrality and Gender Quotas

Abstract Draft
This study examines the influence of network centrality on first-time board appointments in France, focusing on the impact of the gender quota reform of 2010. Using BoardEx data on executives from publicly listed French firms (2001-2019), I construct professional network measures (degree and betweenness) of executives using their employment history. The findings show that while network centrality does not predict board appointments, it plays a greater role for women post-reform, suggesting that increased visibility may help overcome gender barriers. This study contributes to research on networks, corporate governance, and gender quotas by highlighting how legislative changes shape career advancement in leadership.

Stereotypes and Effort Provision in Teams

with H. Ahmed, J. Lamb and A. McCrea – (Data collection ongoing)

Abstract
Effort provision in teamwork can be influenced by stereotypes, particularly in remote work settings where individuals collaborate across locations. Stereotypesmay affect effort based on perceptions of a teammate’s capability or beliefs about how capable teammates perceive them. This study explores the impact of stereotypes and beliefs about stereotypes on teamwork effort using an online experiment based on real effort task.

Other work

Fiscal deficit and inflation: New evidence from Pakistan using a bounds testing approach

with A. Jalil and N. Bibi – Economic Modelling, 2014

Abstract Link
Leeper (1991), Sims (1994) and Woodford (2001) point out that price level is not independently determined by monetary policy rather it is the result of inter dependence of fiscal and monetary policies. This article aims to test the fiscal theory of price level for Pakistan using an autoregressive distributed lag model framework over the period of 1972–2012. The article finds that fiscal deficit is a major determinant of the price level along with other variables like interest rates, government sector borrowing and private borrowing. On the basis of our findings, the present article suggests that the economy of Pakistan requires an immediate correction of fiscal imbalances.

The State of Education during the Covid-19 Crisis

Chapter 10: The state of Pakistan’s Economy: During the Pandemic and Beyond.

with F. Sadik – IBA Press, 2021

Abstract Link
It examines how Covid-19 deepened Pakistan's education crisis, where 22.8 million children were already out of school pre-pandemic. School closures and income losses pushed households into lower income brackets, driving dropouts (16% cited unpaid fees). Poor internet, computer, and TV access, especially in rural areas, undermined the shift to online learning and the "TeleSchool" initiative, worsening educational inequality. Simulations project learning losses of 0.4–0.7 years (LAYS), building on evidence that past disruptions caused lasting setbacks. Returns to education fell across most levels during lockdown, with women and girls facing disproportionate risks. The chapter calls for remediation programs and protected education financing.
