**Work in progress** — [AEA RCT Registry](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/17763)

This paper studies how labour-market information diffuses through social networks when it improves relative performance in competitive hiring. I distinguish *vacancy information*, which signals that a job exists, from *process information*, which improves performance in selection. I study both in a lab-in-the-field experiment with university students in Pakistan, who learn about a real paid job opportunity and decide whether, and with whom, to share it. The design cross-randomises process information, observability, and competition salience.

Process information does not change application behaviour, yet it is withheld far more tightly than the vacancy. Holding it also leads participants to share the vacancy 7 to 9 percentage points less widely, from a 60 percent baseline. Observability raises sharing only without an advantage, while competition salience suppresses advantaged sharing, especially of process information. Dyadic evidence shows that the vacancy travels along friendship ties, whereas process information is guarded even from friends and flows toward central peers. The findings show that networks transmit valuable information selectively, reproducing unequal access and favouring the well-connected over the most able.
